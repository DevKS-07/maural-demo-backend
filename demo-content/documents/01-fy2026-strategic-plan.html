FY2026 Strategic Plan

The Recurring Revenue Transition · Thornbury Energy Group · Prepared for the leadership team and the board · Revised 14 January 2026

1. Executive summary

Thornbury has grown revenue every year since 2015 and will likely finish FY2026 near $29.8M. That is a good number attached to a fragile business. Roughly 88% of what we sell is one-time work: we win a retrofit, we deliver it, and the revenue ends. Every January the company starts again at close to zero and has to re-win its year from a standing start.

This plan commits to changing the shape of the revenue, not just its size. The mechanism is Monitoring-Based Commissioning (MBCx) — annual contracts under which we keep tuning a building after the retrofit is finished, and keep proving the savings. Today MBCx is about $3.24M of trailing-twelve-month revenue, roughly 12.4% of the total. The three-year target is 28%.

The strategic question is not "how do we grow faster". It is "what would make this company worth buying" — and the answer is contracted revenue that survives the founder, the salesperson and the calendar year.

3. The thesis: sell the second decade of the building

A retrofit is a transaction. A building is a relationship. When we finish a retrofit we hand the client a modeled savings figure and walk away, and within eighteen months the building has drifted — setpoints get overridden, schedules get changed, a controls contractor touches something. The savings we were paid to create quietly decay, and nobody is measuring.

MBCx sells the fix as a subscription: continuous monitoring, quarterly tuning, an annual verification report, and a named engineer who knows the building. For the client it protects an investment they already made. For us it converts a project into an annuity.

Why we win this

  • We already hold the baseline. Nobody else can price a monitoring contract on a building we retrofitted as cheaply as we can, because we own the model.
  • Our commissioning agents are on staff, not subcontracted. The competitive set in the Pacific Northwest largely brokers this work out. We do not.
  • We already include 24 months of post-retrofit monitoring in every project. Every retrofit we deliver is a free trial that currently converts by accident rather than by design.

That last point is the cheapest growth available to us and the least well executed. In FY2025 we completed 23 retrofit projects, each with 24 months of monitoring attached, and converted six into paid MBCx contracts at the end of the included period. Nobody owned that conversion. It happened when the project engineer happened to have a good relationship with the facilities director.

5. What has to be true

5.1 Utilization has to reach 72% this year and 74% by 2028

We are at 66.4%. Against 48 billable FTEs, the gap to target is roughly $1.6M of unbilled annual capacity — which is most of the EBITDA miss. This is not a headcount problem. We are paying for the capacity already.

The structural cause is that retrofit delivery is lumpy and our bench has nothing to absorb it. When a construction window slips, the engineers assigned to it go idle. Energy audits are the designated absorber: they are small, schedulable, and they feed the retrofit pipeline. Getting audit work onto the bench inside two weeks of a slip is the single highest-value operating change in this plan.

5.2 Client concentration has to come down

Our top five clients are 58.2% of revenue. Cascadia Health System alone is 19.4%, and its master agreement goes out to competitive rebid in Q1 2027. We should assume we might lose it. A business where one client leaving removes a fifth of revenue is not a business anyone will pay a good multiple for.

5.3 The founder has to stop being the sales team

Dana personally closes 43% of revenue. Every week Dana spends on a proposal is a week not spent on the things only the founder can do, and it is the largest single discount a buyer would apply to this company. The FY2026 commitment is to hire a second senior seller and move founder-closed revenue below 35% by December.

5.4 Cash has to convert faster

DSO is 68 days against a 45-day target, and $1.9M of receivables are past 90 days. The cause is that our billing milestones are tied to construction completion rather than to engineering progress, so we finance our clients' schedule slips.

5.5 We have to stop losing what we know

Fifteen years of baselines, sequences of operation, commissioning reports and savings models live in individual engineers' folders. At 14% attrition, we lose a meaningful part of our own institutional memory every year — and the buildings we know best are the ones we are least able to staff flexibly, because only one person knows them. The knowledge management platform we adopted in 2026 is the response; its value is not document storage but the fact that a second engineer can pick up a building without a two-week handover that never happens.

7. Risks

  • Cascadia rebid (Q1 2027). 19.4% of revenue in one competitive process. We should be planning for the loss, not the win.
  • Founder dependency. Concentrated in sales (43% of closed revenue) and, to a lesser extent, delivery (18%). This is a key-person risk that a buyer prices explicitly.
  • Hiring. Commissioning engineers are genuinely scarce. Nine open roles at 84 days to fill means the utilization target and the growth target compete for the same people.
  • MBCx is a different sale. It is an operating-budget subscription, not a capital project. The buyer inside the client organisation is often not the same person, and our sellers have not had to hold that conversation before.

8. What the leadership team owns

  1. Marcus Oyelaran — the MBCx delivery standard, and a conversion play for every retrofit reaching the end of its included 24-month monitoring period.
  2. Dana Thornbury — hire the second senior seller; hand over named accounts on a published schedule rather than opportunistically.
  3. Finance — restructure billing milestones onto engineering progress, and clear the aged receivable balance to under $0.8M by year end.
  4. Operations — a standing rule that any project slip of more than two weeks triggers an audit backfill request within five business days.
